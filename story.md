## 1. We started with a question about memory

A movie recommender can learn a user's usual tastes from years of ratings. A handful of recent ratings might suggest the user is in the mood for something different. The project asked whether giving the recommender separate long-term and recent memories could help it respond to that change while maintaining ranking quality, improving the distribution of recommendations and making individual decisions easier to explain. Those became four research questions about accuracy, fairness, explanation fidelity and reliability. [Original project brief and team plan](../references/context.md)

The team planned a Colab prototype alongside three conventional baselines. Popularity ranked films by how often they had been rated. ItemKNN used similarities between films to personalise rankings, and SVD represented users and movies in a lower-dimensional space. Model C used SVD movie vectors to combine long-term preferences with candidate-specific attention over recent positive ratings. A feedback gate adjusted how much recent memory mattered in response to prediction error. Model D added an exploration bonus for films with fewer prior impressions. The locally available `origin/dev` revision already contained the modular implementation, so the project advanced from that starting point. [Model design and project ownership](PROJECT_REPORT.md)

## 2. The first advance was fixing the clock

The original plan split each user's history chronologically. That protected the order of each person's ratings, yet another user's later ratings could still enter the shared model before an earlier test prediction. About **39.9%** of the training ratings were later than the average test event under that protocol. We moved the main evaluation to dataset-wide cutoffs, kept ratings from the same timestamp together at each boundary, and selected settings on validation data before testing. Each model ranked the same set of eligible unseen films. The original split became a sensitivity check. [Temporal diagnostics](results/stage1/20261125-151002-345701Z/temporal_report.yaml)

That choice also changed who we could evaluate. MovieLens 100K contains **943 users**, each with at least 20 ratings across the full dataset. Of the 226 users active in the late test window, 91 had also rated before its cutoff. Seventy-six had at least one four- or five-star test rating for a film already in the fitted catalogue. Those 76 users formed the ranking cohort. The distinction matters because the result describes people with usable history and an eligible positive target at that cutoff. [Dataset and cohort definitions](PROJECT_REPORT.md#data-and-experimental-protocol) · [Test cohort counts](results/stage1/20261125-151002-345701Z/cohorts.yaml)

## 3. The original benchmark separated four kinds of success

On the first global test, Model C reached **0.1825 NDCG@10**, compared with **0.1638** for ItemKNN. The paired interval included zero. Under the original per-user split, ItemKNN had the higher average. Model D broadened exposure and brought it closer to the distribution of relevant rated films across popularity groups. Its long-tail recall stayed at **0.25%**, illustrating how a change in visibility can leave retrieval of relevant films unchanged. [Main benchmark](results/stage3/20261125-151403-405809Z/metrics.csv) · [Per-user sensitivity](results/per_user/sensitivity/20261125-152310-683274Z/metrics.csv)

We then removed individual mechanisms to see which ones mattered. Removing recent memory or replacing candidate-specific attention with uniform weights lowered ranking quality in the original ablation. Deletion tests showed that highly attended movies often mattered to the top recommendation, and attention ranked the full set of evidence poorly. That difference became the demo. Removing *Star Wars* displaced *Return of the Jedi* at the top of one list. In another example, removing *Circle of Friends* raised the score for *The Shawshank Redemption*. Simulated changes in taste revealed a similar trade-off. The cognitive models could respond quickly while losing relevance on earlier interests and becoming more sensitive to noise. [Component ablations](results/stage4/20261125-151958-692581Z/ablation.csv) · [Explanation demo cases](results/temporal_followup/20261125-160227-772960Z/demo_examples.csv)

## 4. Stronger baselines changed the accuracy story

One cutoff left a practical question about whether the result would repeat. We evaluated three separate test windows with **99, 104 and 76** eligible ranking users, tuning each model on an earlier validation period. Popularity, ItemKNN and SVD stayed in the comparison. We added EASE, a lightweight collaborative model that learns how past likes predict other films, and two models with the same movie representation as C that isolated the effect of simple recency weighting.

EASE outscored C on average in all three windows and led logged replay in each. The paired one-shot intervals still included zero. The recency profile responded most strongly to the late simulated shift and retained only **40.8%** of its clean NDCG after five noisy ratings. Those comparisons made the research question more precise. EASE tested how competitive the cognitive models were at ranking, while the recency profile tested how much of their behaviour a simpler memory rule could explain. [Rolling windows](results/temporal_followup/20261125-160227-772960Z/windows.csv) · [Ranking and replay results](results/temporal_followup/20261125-160227-772960Z/metrics.csv) · [Stress tests](results/temporal_followup/20261125-160227-772960Z/anchor_reliability.csv)

## 5. The final experiment separated exploration from the scorer

We applied the same exposure bonus to EASE and Cognitive, tuning the bonus separately under a shared **10% relative validation NDCG loss budget**. Both scorers gained coverage and improved exposure alignment in every test window. The late window gave the clearest example of why the budget needed to be reported separately from test performance. EASE gained **7.71 percentage points** of coverage at an **18.0% relative test NDCG loss**. Cognitive gained **1.58 points** at a **1.1% loss**. Tail recall changed little and moved in different directions. Exploration worked with both scorers, and its accuracy cost depended on the scorer and the window. The project began by asking whether recent memory could improve a movie recommender. It ended with evidence about where it helps and what it costs. Memory and attention affected ranking and responsiveness under controlled changes. Exploration broadened exposure with both a cognitive model and a simpler collaborative model. These findings apply to the tested MovieLens cohorts and offline scenarios. A prospective evaluation or a user study would address how people experience the recommendations and their explanations.
